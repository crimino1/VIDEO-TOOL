TITLE
5 Weird Computers That Are Alive (Slime Mold, DNA, Brain Cells)

DESCRIPTION
A slime mold with no brain rebuilt the Tokyo rail network. A test tube solved a routing puzzle with DNA. One gram of DNA can hold 215 petabytes. Bacteria can run code compiled from Verilog. And about 800,000 brain cells in a dish learned to play Pong. This is weird computer science: five living computers, each one stranger than the last.

A computer is anything that takes in information, follows rules and gives you an answer. By that definition, some of the strangest computers on Earth are alive. In this documentary-style explainer you'll see the slime mold Tokyo rail network experiment, the first DNA computer and why it scales so badly, DNA data storage explained (an operating system and a short film stored in DNA with zero errors), MIT's Cello compiler that programs E. coli bacteria, and DishBrain, the lab-grown brain cells that play Pong, plus why your brain runs on about 20 watts while AI needs a whole data center.

The hardware got weird. The ideas stayed exactly the same: shortest paths, parallel search, error correction, logic gates and learning from feedback. That's why the fundamentals are worth learning properly. Bit by bit.
