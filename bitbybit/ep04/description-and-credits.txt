DESCRIPTION
A slime mold with no brain rebuilt the Tokyo rail network. A test tube solved a routing puzzle with DNA. A gram of DNA can hold 215 petabytes. Bacteria can run code compiled from Verilog. And about 800,000 brain cells in a dish learned to play Pong. This is weird computer science: five living computers, each one stranger than the last, and the one thing they all have in common.

A computer is anything that takes in information, follows rules and gives you an answer. By that definition some of the strangest computers on Earth are alive. In this documentary-style explainer: Physarum polycephalum and the 2010 Tokyo rail experiment (Tero, Nakagaki et al.), Leonard Adleman's 1994 DNA computer and why it scales so badly, DNA data storage (Erlich and Zielinski, 2017: an operating system and a short film stored in DNA with zero errors), MIT's Cello compiler that turns Verilog into DNA for E. coli (2016), and Cortical Labs' DishBrain (2022), the neurons that learned Pong, plus why a brain runs on about 20 watts.

The hardware got weird. The ideas stayed exactly the same: shortest paths, parallel search, error correction, logic gates, learning from feedback. That's why the fundamentals are worth learning properly. Bit by bit.
