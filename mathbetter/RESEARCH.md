## 1. George Dantzig: Berkeley, 1939 [0:00-0:39, 11:26-11:40]
**Primary source:** Donald J. Albers & Constance Reid, "An Interview with George B. Dantzig: The Father of Linear Programming",
*The College Mathematics Journal* 17(4), Sept 1986, pp. 292-314 (JSTOR 2686279). Interview held at Stanford, Nov 1984. Copy in research_src/; receipt image is `ss_dantzig_cmj1986_p301.png`.
What Dantzig says (p. 301):
- "during my first year at Berkeley I arrived late one day at one of Neyman's classes." He went to Berkeley in 1939 to do his PhD under Neyman (p. 300). OK
- There were two problems on the blackboard and he assumed they were homework. OK
- "A few days later I apologized to Neyman for taking so long to do the homework - the problems seemed to be a little harder to do than usual." OK
- "About six weeks later, one Sunday morning about eight o'clock, Anne [his wife] and I were awakened by someone banging on our front door. It was Neyman." OK
- The problems "were in fact two famous unsolved problems in statistics". A year later Neyman accepted them as his thesis. The second problem was published around 1950 with Abraham Wald (Wald added Dantzig as co-author on the galley proofs). OK
- **FLAG "about twenty minutes late":** the 1986 interview says only "arrived late". The 20 minutes is not in his own account. Say "late", or keep 20 minutes as an unsourced retelling detail.
- **NUANCE "put on the board as examples of what nobody had cracked":** Dantzig doesn't say why Neyman wrote them up. He only says they were famous unsolved problems. Safer: "They were famous unsolved problems in statistics."
- Bonus detail from the same interview (p. 293): Dantzig was "flunking" his first algebra course in junior high school. This fits the video's theme.
- **Simplex method:** "In 1947 Dantzig invented linear programming and the simplex method" (p. 293). The interview names uses in steel, refinery scheduling, energy policy and assigning personnel. Airline crew/fleet scheduling and factory production planning are standard uses of linear programming, so "still used to plan airline schedules and factory output" is fair. NUANCE: it's linear programming solved by the simplex method or its successors (today often interior-point solvers). "A method that's still used" is acceptable.
- Life dates: 1914-2005. Photos: 1936 portrait (PD) and the 1976 National Medal of Science ceremony (White House photo, PD).

## 2. Laurent Schwartz [1:23-2:06]
- Fields Medal **1950**, shared with Atle Selberg (IMU Fields Medal list), for the theory of **distributions** (generalized functions; this put the Dirac delta and related tools used by physicists on a rigorous footing). OK
- Memoir: *Un mathématicien aux prises avec le siècle* (Odile Jacob, 1997). English: ***A Mathematician Grappling with His Century***, translated by Leila Schneps (Birkhäuser, 2001). OK
- The passage is widely quoted (e.g., Jo Boaler, Stanford/youcubed) in this translation: when a new question came up, less gifted students answered faster than he did. "Towards the end of the eleventh grade, I secretly thought of myself as stupid. (The blog summary adds that he worried his supposed intelligence would be exposed as an illusion. That is a paraphrase; the exact wording is unverified.)" Then: "I need time to seize things because I always need to understand them fully". He concludes that "rapidity doesn't have a precise relationship to intelligence". The narration matches this. OK
- NUANCE: I couldn't open the book itself, so the page number is unverified and the wording above is from the Boaler/Wikiquote quotation. "11th grade" is the translator's rendering of French *classe de première*, so "11th grade" is fine on screen.

## 3. Maryam Mirzakhani [2:06-2:38]
Source: Erica Klarreich, "A Tenacious Explorer of Abstract Surfaces", *Quanta Magazine*, 12 Aug 2014.
- Grew up in Tehran and went to Farzanegan middle and high schools for girls. "To her dismay, Mirzakhani did poorly in her mathematics class that year", which was her first year of middle school. Her teacher didn't think she was talented, and this hurt her confidence. The next year a more encouraging teacher turned it around. OK. "Around age 12" is fine: she was born 1977 and started middle school at about 11-12.
- **First woman to win the Fields Medal: 2014** (Quanta; AFP). The medal was presented at ICM Seoul on 13 Aug 2014 (date of the Commons ceremony photos). AFP (15 July 2017): born 1977, raised in Tehran, Stanford professor from 2008, died of cancer in July 2017 aged 40. OK
- "lost in a jungle": "It is like being lost in a jungle and trying to use all the knowledge that you can gather to come up with some new tricks, and with some luck you might find a way out." Quoted in AFP's 2017 obituary (phys.org, 15 July 2017) and in Quartz. It's usually traced to a 2014 Stanford News interview, but stanford.edu blocked my fetch, so the original source is unverified. It is **not** in her 2008 Clay interview. OK as a quote.
- She describes herself as slow. Clay Mathematics Institute 2008 interview: "I am a slow thinker". Quanta: "Mirzakhani likes to describe herself as slow." OK
- NUANCE [2:23] "She described her own work as slow, like being lost in a jungle...": these are two separate remarks. "Slow" is her self-description (Clay, Quanta). The jungle line is about how she develops new proofs. Fine as narration, but don't put them on screen as one quote.
- Big paper and daughter: Quanta says she doodles on giant sheets of paper on the floor. Her 3-year-old daughter **Anahita** often exclaims, "Oh, Mommy is painting again!" OK
- Video: in the Simons Foundation profile (2019), she draws on large paper at about 2:24-2:40.

## 4. Lyons & Beilock 2012, "When Math Hurts" [2:51-3:31]
Ian M. Lyons & Sian L. Beilock, "When Math Hurts: Math Anxiety Predicts Pain Network Activation in Anticipation of Doing Math", *PLoS ONE* 7(10): e48076, published **31 Oct 2012**. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0048076. **CC BY**, so Figure 1 can be used with credit.
- University of Chicago (Lyons was also at Western University, Ontario). fMRI with 28 adults: 14 high and 14 low math-anxious. OK
- Higher math anxiety predicted more activity, while anticipating a math task, in bilateral **dorso-posterior insula** (an area tied to visceral threat and pain), mid-cingulate cortex and right dorsal central sulcus. OK
- Abstract: "this relation was not seen during math performance, suggesting that it is not that math itself hurts; rather, the anticipation of math is painful." OK
- NUANCE "Once they were actually doing the problems, it disappeared": the paper says the correlation with anxiety was not seen during math performance. In Fig. 1 the right-insula "math-task" bar is still about .39, but not significant. "It wasn't there" or "didn't show up" is safer than "disappeared".
- NUANCE "In the anxious group ... the more anxious the person, the stronger": correct. The effect was found within the high-anxiety group (n = 14, a small sample).

## 5. Ramirez & Beilock 2011, expressive writing [3:45-4:13]
Gerardo Ramirez & Sian L. Beilock, "Writing About Testing Worries Boosts Exam Performance in the Classroom", *Science* 331(6014): 211-213, Jan 2011. doi:10.1126/science.1199427. Details from the abstract and Ed Yong's National Geographic write-up.
- Two lab experiments (college students, a pressure manipulation, **math** tests) and two randomized field experiments with **ninth graders before their high-school biology final**. They wrote for 10 minutes right before the exam.
- **FLAG (context):** the classroom study used a **biology** final, not a math exam. The narration only says "a real final exam", which is technically right. In a math video, avoid math visuals there or say "a biology final".
- **FLAG "beat anxious students who sat quietly":** only lab Experiment 1 used a sit-quietly control. In the classroom studies the control group **wrote about an unrelated topic**. Lab Experiment 2 also had an unrelated-writing control. Suggested wording: "beat anxious students who didn't write about their worries."
- NUANCE "anxious students spent 10 minutes writing": in the classroom, *all* students were randomly assigned to write. The benefit showed up for the highly test-anxious, while low-anxiety students did about the same either way.
- NOTE: Europe PMC shows "This article has been corrected. See Science. 2014 Apr 11;344(6180):151". I couldn't read the correction text (science.org blocked). A 2011 comment by Lang & Lang and the authors' reply are in Science 332:791-792. Treat the result as published and peer-reviewed. Don't call it "proven".

## 6. Gauss, 1 to 100 [4:15-6:13]
- Brian Hayes, "Gauss's Day of Reckoning", *American Scientist* 94(3): 200-205, May-June 2006.
- Copy in research_src/hayes2006_gauss_day_of_reckoning.pdf (+ .txt). Hayes collected "over a hundred exemplars, in eight languages".
- The earliest source is Wolfgang Sartorius von Waltershausen's memorial biography *Gauss zum Gedächtniss* (1856). Gauss entered Büttner's school in Braunschweig "in 1784 after his seventh birthday", stayed two years "without special incident", then reached the arithmetic class. So he was about 9, around 1786. His teacher was J. G. Büttner, with about a hundred pupils, a switch, and slates piled on a table. Büttner set "eine arithmetische Reihe" (an arithmetic series) to sum. **No 1-100.** Gauss threw down his slate saying "There it lies" (in Braunschweig dialect) and had the only correct answer. The "1 to 100" was inserted by Helen Worthington Gauss's English translation and later retellings.
- The narration already says "Its details change from teller to teller", which is good. NUANCE: Sartorius does say "At the end of the hour the slates were turned", so the hour is in the original. "Meant to keep them quiet" is a later embellishment. Keep it framed as legend.
- Math check: 50 x 101 = 5050, n(n+1)/2, and 500 x 1001 = 500,500. OK. Note the TTS glitch at 5:37 ("500 times 1,001 is 500 times 1,001 is 500 times"); the line needs to say "500,500".

## 7. Robert Bjork, "desirable difficulties" [6:28-6:41]
- Robert A. Bjork, UCLA (Bjork Learning and Forgetting Lab). He coined the term in **1994**: "Memory and metamemory considerations in the training of human beings", in J. Metcalfe & A. Shimamura (eds.), *Metacognition: Knowing about Knowing*, MIT Press, pp. 185-205. OK. The established term is plural, "desirable difficulties". The Bjorks stress that not every difficulty is desirable.

## 8. Roediger & Karpicke 2006, testing effect [6:41-7:07]
Henry L. Roediger III & Jeffrey D. Karpicke, "Test-Enhanced Learning: Taking Memory Tests Improves Long-Term Retention", *Psychological Science* 17(3): 249-255, March 2006 (Washington University in St. Louis). Copy in research_src/.
- Materials: two short prose passages from a TOEFL prep book, "The Sun" (256 words) and "Sea Otters" (275 words). OK ("a short passage")
- Exp. 1 (n = 120): study + restudy vs study + test. **5 min: restudy 81% vs test 75%. 2 days: 54% vs 68%. 1 week: 42% vs 56%.**
- Exp. 2 (n = 180) matches the narration's design best. SSSS read the passage 4 times (about 14 full readings); STTT read it **once**, then took 3 free-recall tests. **5 min: SSSS 83%, SSST 78%, STTT 71%. 1 week: STTT 61%, SSST 56%, SSSS 40%.** OK ("remembered far more")
- Confidence: the re-reading group predicted better 1-week memory (7-point scale: 4.8 vs 4.2 for SSST and 4.0 for STTT). OK ("The re-readers felt more confident")
- Optional honesty beat: after **5 minutes** the re-readers were ahead. The reversal only appears with delay.

## 9. Ebbinghaus 1885 [7:09-7:35]
Hermann Ebbinghaus, *Über das Gedächtnis: Untersuchungen zur experimentellen Psychologie* (Leipzig: Duncker & Humblot, 1885). English: *Memory*, trans. H. A. Ruger & C. E. Bussenius, 1913 (psychclassics.yorku.ca).
- He was his own only subject. He learned lists of nonsense syllables (13 syllables per row in the forgetting series) and measured **savings**: the percentage of the original learning time saved when relearning. OK
- Table on p. 103 (receipt image: `ebbinghaus_1885_p103_savings_table.jpg`), savings Q after x hours:
  0.33 h = 58.2% / 1 h = 44.2% / 8.8 h = 35.8% / 24 h = 33.7% / 48 h = 27.8% / 6 days = 25.4% / 31 days = 21.1%.
  So more than half was effectively lost within an hour and about two-thirds within a day. "Most of what he learned slipped away within the first day" is OK, but it is savings when relearning, not recall.
- **FLAG (visual):** the forgetting results (§§27-29) are given as a **table**, not a graph. The Commons 1885 figures I opened (Fig. 2 and Fig. 4) plot fluctuations between test series, not forgetting, so do not label them the forgetting curve. (Fig. 6 was not checked.) The famous curve is a later redrawing of the table. Use the p. 103 table scan as the "original", and draw the curve yourselves from the numbers above, labelled "data: Ebbinghaus 1885".
- "Each time he reviewed, the forgetting slowed down": ch. 8 (Ruger trans.) says that with daily relearning each repetition needed less work and less was forgotten. OK
- **NUANCE "three short sessions spread across a week beat one cram":** Ebbinghaus's own spacing result was that 38 repetitions spread over 3 days were as effective as 68 repetitions in one day. "Across a week" and "very different result" come from later spacing research. Suggest "spread over a few days", or credit the claim to spacing research in general.

## 10. Rohrer, Dedrick & Burgess 2014, interleaving [7:38-8:21]
**Doug Rohrer, Robert F. Dedrick & Kaleena Burgess, "The benefit of interleaved mathematics practice is not limited to superficially similar kinds of problems", *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 21(5): 1323-1330, Oct 2014.** doi:10.3758/s13423-014-0588-3. **This is the 72% vs 38% paper.** Copy in research_src/.
(A separate, later Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic 2015 study in *J. Educational Psychology* exists. That citation is from memory and not re-checked.)
- A public middle school in Tampa, Florida: 3 teachers, 8 seventh-grade classes, 157 participants, 140 analysed. Nine-week practice phase with 10 assignments. **Unannounced test two weeks later.** Interleaved 72% vs blocked 38%, d = 1.05. OK
- Four problem kinds: solving linear equations, proportion word problems, graphing y = mx + b, and slope from two points (Fig. 4; receipt image `ss_rohrer_2014_results.png`).
- **FLAG "The shuffled group scored 72%. The block group scored 38":** this was a **counterbalanced crossover**, not two groups. Every student interleaved two problem kinds and blocked the other two. 72% vs 38% compares the **same students** on interleaved vs blocked material. Suggested wording: "On problems they had practised shuffled, students scored 72%. On problems practised in blocks, 38%."
- **FLAG "During practice, the shuffled group found it harder":** this paper doesn't report practice difficulty. Assignment scores weren't used as a measure, because students corrected their work in class. Earlier lab studies (I believe Rohrer & Taylor 2007, *Instructional Science*; not re-checked) did find lower practice performance under interleaving. Either drop the line or credit it to "earlier studies".

## 11. Negative times negative [8:28-9:16]
The forward/backward walking video run in reverse is a standard teaching analogy for the sign rule. It isn't a research claim, so no source is needed. The math is fine. (The formal reason is distributivity: (-1)(-1) + (-1)(1) = (-1)(0) = 0.)

## 12. Andrew Wiles and Fermat's Last Theorem [9:20-10:51]
Sources: NOVA "The Proof" transcript (PBS, aired 28 Oct 1997; directed by Simon Singh, written and produced by John Lynch; UK original BBC Horizon "Fermat's Last Theorem", 1996). IMU/Abel Prize 2016 biography of Wiles (copy in research_src/). plus.maths.org (Univ. of Cambridge), "A very old problem turns 30". Oxford Mathematics clip "When did you know it was Maths?" (2023).
- Age 10, library book: 1963, Cambridge, England, "his local library" (Abel bio). In the Oxford clip at 0:00-0:20 Wiles says: "I got a book out of the public library, E.T. Bell, called The Last Problem, and I was just hooked." OK
- Started in **1986** (Abel bio: "A turning point came in 1986"). NOVA: Ken Ribet showed that Taniyama-Shimura implies Fermat, and Wiles then went back to the problem. OK
- Secrecy: Abel bio: "The only person he confided in was his wife, Nada, who he married shortly after embarking on the proof." "After seven years of intense and secret study". OK
- **NUANCE "Only his wife knew":** NOVA says he told Nick Katz in January 1993 (and Katz then sat through his private lecture course). So "only his wife knew" holds for almost the whole time. "For years, only his wife knew" is fully safe.
- NUANCE "attic": the attic study at his Princeton home is in Singh's book and widely repeated. It isn't in the NOVA transcript or the Abel bio. Fine as narration.
- Announcement: **Wednesday 23 June 1993**, at the end of a lecture series at the **Isaac Newton Institute, Cambridge** (plus.maths.org). His closing words: "I think I'll stop here." OK
- The gap: a referee found the error. NOVA: Nick Katz refereed the manuscript line by line in July-August 1993, and his question "turned into the error". Wiles: the flaw became clear "at the end of September" 1993. OK
- Richard Taylor, his **former PhD student**, came to Princeton to help from early 1994. OK
- The fix came on **Monday morning, 19 September 1994** (NOVA; 19 Sept 1994 was a Monday). OK. Quote: "It was the most - the most important moment of my working life." OK
- Published in *Annals of Mathematics*, 1995: Wiles, "Modular elliptic curves and Fermat's Last Theorem" (Abel bio). The companion Taylor & Wiles paper and the volume number (141) are cited from memory and not re-checked.
- Dark mansion quote (NOVA transcript, saved as research_src/nova_the_proof_transcript.txt): "...entering a dark mansion. One goes into the first room, and it's dark, completely dark. One stumbles around bumping into the furniture... finally, after six months or so, you find the light switch. You turn it on, and suddenly, it's all illuminated. You can see exactly where you were." OK. NUANCE: the NOVA cut stops there. The "then you move into the next room" continuation appears in longer versions (e.g., Singh's book) that I didn't verify here. The narration's "next dark room" is fine as paraphrase but unsourced in NOVA.
- "350 years": Fermat wrote his margin note around 1637, and the proof was finished in 1994, about 357 years. "More than 350 years" is the usual phrase. OK
- Clip (plusmathsorg, 2016, Heidelberg Laureate Forum) at about 1:00-2:20: Wiles says adults must accept "this state of being stuck ... everybody goes through it" and criticises the Good Will Hunting "born with it" message. This fits the ending.
